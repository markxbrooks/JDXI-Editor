Part V is an especially interesting example of Jarre's early sound design because it combines quite simple synthesizer waveforms with elaborate modulation, filtering, sequencing and spatial effects.

The sound world is distinctly analogue: rather than relying on samples of recognisable instruments, Jarre creates the impression of organic, almost biological activity using oscillators and electronic modulation.

## 1. The opening bubbling/throbbing sound

The sound you're asking about

The opening sound is best understood as a modulated analogue synthesizer tone, rather than a conventional percussion instrument.

Its character suggests several processes working together:

- Low-frequency amplitude modulation: the volume pulses, creating the throbbing quality.
- Filter modulation: the tone changes in brightness, giving it the bubbling, liquid quality.
- Oscillator interaction: slightly different frequencies can produce beating, making the sound seem to breathe or fluctuate.
- Resonance: a resonant low-pass filter can emphasise a moving frequency region, creating the impression of bubbles or gurgling.

One particularly plausible technique is a low-frequency oscillator (LFO) controlling the filter cutoff, possibly combined with amplitude modulation.

The result is a sound that seems to be alive rather than simply repeating.

Importantly, I would not identify it as a single specific synthesizer or circuit without an isolated recording or production documentation. The audible character is consistent with the analogue synthesis techniques Jarre was using in this period.

There is an important distinction here: Part V has a characteristic repeating keyboard sequence, and this is not simply an LFO-generated effect.

The documented main sequence was produced using the RMI Keyboard Computer. The track also uses the RMI Harmonic Synthesizer. The RMI sequence is therefore a particularly important part of its identity.&#x20;

I would describe the opening as having two perceptual characteristics:

The latter is especially important: the repetition is part of the musical sequencing, not necessarily an effect generated by a free-running LFO.

## 2. How the track evolves

I hear the composition as developing through several layers rather than following a conventional verse/chorus structure.

| Stage             | Sound design                                                       | Effect                                          |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Opening           | Liquid, pulsing electronic textures and repeating notes            | Creates an underwater, organic atmosphere       |
| Early development | Sustained harmonic layers and additional repeating patterns        | Establishes depth and movement                  |
| Middle            | A darker, more prominent bass pattern emerges                      | Introduces tension and forward momentum         |
| Build-up          | More insistent sequences and increasingly dense synthesizer layers | Moves from ambient to rhythmic                  |
| Later section     | Stronger melodic and rhythmic interplay                            | Creates a more energetic, almost cosmic journey |
| Ending            | Textures gradually lose their rhythmic definition                  | Returns to an atmospheric, fluid soundscape     |

This is a particularly effective compositional technique: Jarre starts with sounds that have no obvious physical identity, then gradually makes them behave like a musical environment.

The bass sequence is especially important. It changes the listener's perception of the earlier material: what initially sounded like abstract electronic activity begins to feel like a rhythmic organism.

## 4. How I would recreate the opening sound

Given your work with the JD-Xi and MIDI transcription, I would approach this as a sound-design exercise rather than trying to reproduce it with a conventional preset.

Suggested synthesis architecture

Oscillator

Triangle / sawtooth / pulse waveform

Low-pass filter

Moderate resonance, moving cutoff

Amplitude and filter modulation

Slow LFOs, potentially at different rates

Delay / phasing

Create movement and spatial depth

Liquid, bubbling electronic texture

I would experiment with three separate modulation sources:

- LFO 1 controlling filter cutoff.
- LFO 2 controlling amplitude, at a different rate.
- A sequenced pitch pattern controlling the actual notes.

The combination is much more convincing than simply applying tremolo to a synthesizer preset.

## 5. My overall interpretation

What makes Part V particularly impressive is its transition between three musical states:

1. Organic: sounds that resemble water, bubbles and biological activity.
2. Mechanical: repeating sequences and pulsing patterns establish a machine-like rhythm.
3. Cosmic: sustained harmonies, bass movement and spatial effects create an expansive, almost cinematic atmosphere.

It is a wonderful example of how electronic sound design can create the impression of a physical environment without using environmental recordings.

One distinction I'd particularly like to investigate: whether the initial bubbling sound is primarily the RMI sequence's timbre or a separate modulated synthesizer layer. The published equipment information establishes the RMI's role in the sequence, but doesn't conclusively identify every individual sound in the opening.

For a reconstruction in your JD-Xi, separating the sequenced notes from the modulated timbre would be the most useful first step.
